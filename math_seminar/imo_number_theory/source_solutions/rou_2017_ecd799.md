## Supplied solution 1

To rule out all other values of $n$, it is sufficient to exhibit a monic polynomial $P$ of degree at most $n$ with integer coefficients, whose restriction to the integers is injective, and $P(x) \equiv 1 \pmod{n}$ for all integers $x$. This is easily seen by reading the relation in the statement modulo $n$, to deduce that $k \equiv 1 \pmod{n}$, so $k = 1$, since $1 \le k \le n$; hence $P(x_1) = P(x_2)$ for some distinct integers $x_1$ and $x_2$, which contradicts injectivity.
If $n = 1$, let $P = X$, and if $n = 4$, let $P = X^4 + 7X^2 + 4X + 1$. In the latter case, clearly, $P(x) \equiv 1 \pmod 4$ for all integers $x$; and $P$ is injective on the integers, since $P(x) - P(y) = (x - y)((x + y)(x^2 + y^2 + 7) + 4)$, and the absolute value of $(x + y)(x^2 + y^2 + 7)$ is either 0 or at least 7 for integral $x$ and $y$.

Finally, let $P = f_n + nX + 1$ if $n$ is odd, and let $P = f_{n-1} + nX + 1$ if $n$ is even. In either case, $P$ is strictly increasing, hence injective, on the integers, and $P(x) \equiv 1 \pmod{n}$ for all integers $x$.
