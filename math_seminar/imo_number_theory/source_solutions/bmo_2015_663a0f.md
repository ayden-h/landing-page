## Supplied solution 1

We prove that $(x, y) = (2^n, 2^n)$ or $(x, y) = (3^n, 2 \cdot 3^n)$ or $(x, y) = (2 \cdot 3^n, 3^n)$ for a natural number $n$. We can easily check those solutions.

**Step 1:** The number $x^3 + y^3$ has at most one odd prime divisor. Let $p_1 < p_2 < \cdots < p_k$ be the odd prime divisors of $x^3 + y^3$ with $k \ge 2$. Then the numbers $p_1$ and $p_2 \cdots p_k$ are relatively prime divisors of $x^3 + y^3$. Thus, $N_1 = p_1 + (p_2 \cdots p_k) - 1$ is a divisor of $x^3 + y^3$. Because $N_1$ is odd, we have that
$$
N_1 = p_1^{r_1} \cdots p_k^{r_k}
$$
for some natural numbers $r_1, \dots, r_k$. But, for $i \ge 2$, the number $p_i$ can't be a divisor of $N_1$ since then $p_i$ would be a divisor of $p_1 - 1$, which is a contradiction, because $p_i > p_1$.
Therefore, $N_1 = p_1^{m_1}$ and $m_1 > 1$ since $N_1 > p_1$. Additionally, the number
$$
N_2 = p_1^2 + (p_2 \cdots p_k) - 1,
$$
is also a divisor of $x^3 + y^3$. A similar argument as above gives us that $N_2 = p_1^{m_2}$. So, we have that
$$
p_1^{m_2} - p_1^{m_1} = N_2 - N_1 = p_1^2 - p_1.
$$
Of course we have that $m_2 > m_1$ but, this gives us
$$
p_1^{m_2} - p_1^{m_1} \ge p_1^{m_1}(p_1 - 1) \ge p_1(p_1 - 1) = p_1^2 - p_1,
$$
where the equality holds if and only if $m_1 = 1, m_2 = 2$. But we have already seen that $m_1 > 1$ so we reach a contradiction and thus $x^3 + y^3$ has at most one odd prime divisor.

**Step 2:** The number $x^3 + y^3$ has at most one prime divisor.
Suppose that this is not the case, then from the first step $x^3 + y^3$ has exactly two prime divisors, the number $2$ and some odd prime number $p$.
Then, $2 + p - 1 = p + 1$ divides $x^3 + y^3$. Since $(p, p + 1) = 1$, as in the first step, $p + 1$ has to be a power of $2$, thus $4$ divides $x^3 + y^3$. Furthermore, $4 + p - 1 = p + 3$ also divides $x^3 + y^3$. But this means that if $p \ne 3$ then $p + 3$ is also a power of $2$. But if $p > 3$, then $p + 1, p + 3$ cannot both be powers of $2$. Therefore, $p = 3$, and $x^3 + y^3 = 2^a \cdot 3^b$ for some positive integers $a, b$.
If $a \ge 3$, then $8 | x^3 + y^3$ so $8 + 3 - 1 = 10$ divides $x^3 + y^3$, which is a contradiction.
If $b \ge 2$, then $9 | x^3 + y^3$, so again $9 + 2 - 1 = 10$ divides $x^3 + y^3$, which is again a contradiction.
Therefore, $x^3 + y^3 \in \{6, 12\}$ but the last one has no solutions.
By using Steps 1 and 2, we have only to solve the equation
$$
x^3 + y^3 = p^a, \qquad (15)
$$
where $p$ is a prime number and $a$ is a positive integer. We write $x = p^s m$ and $y = p^t n$ where $m, n$ are positive integers which are not divisible by $p$. It is evident that they are also coprime and (15) can be rewritten in the form
$$
m^3 + n^3 = p^b \implies (m + n)(m^2 - m n + n^2) = p^b.
$$
However,
$$
\text{gcd}(m + n, m^2 - m n + n^2) = \text{gcd}(m + n, 3 m n) = 1 \text{ or } 3.
$$
In the first case, since $m + n > 1$, we get that $m^2 - m n + n^2 = 1$ or $m = n = 1$, which gives us $p = 2$ and $x = y = 2^n$ for some non-negative integer $n$.
In the second case, we have that $p = 3$ and either $m + n = 3$ or $m^2 - m n + n^2 = 3$. As a result, $(m, n) \in \{(2, 1), (1, 2)\}$, so $x = 2 \cdot 3^n, y = 3^n$ or $x = 3^n, y = 2 \cdot 3^n$ for non-negative integer $n$.
$\square$
