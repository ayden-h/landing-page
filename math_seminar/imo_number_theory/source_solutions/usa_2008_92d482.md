## Supplied solution 1

Assume on the contrary that $n^7 + 7 = m^2$ for some integer $m$.
For $n$ even, $n^7 + 7 \equiv 3 \pmod 4$, so it cannot be a perfect square. For $n \equiv 3 \pmod 4$, $n^7 + 7 \equiv 2 \pmod 4$, so again it cannot be a perfect square. We must have $n \equiv 1 \pmod 4$ and $m$ even.

Note that $n^7 + 2^7 = n^7 + 7 + 121 = m^2 + 11^2$. Thus $n+2$ is a divisor of $m^2 + 11^2$. But $n+2 \equiv 3 \pmod 4$, and if $\gcd(m, 11) = 1$, this is impossible by a well-known fact about sums of two squares. We must have $\gcd(m, 11) = 11$, then $m = 11m_1$ for some even integer $m_1$ and
$$
(n+2)(n^6 - 2n^5 + 2^2 \cdot n^4 - 2^3 \cdot n^3 + 2^4 \cdot n^2 - 2^5 \cdot n + 2^6) = n^7 + 2^7 = 11^2(m_1^2 + 1).
$$
Since $n+2 \equiv 3 \pmod 4$ and $m_1^2 + 1 \equiv 1 \pmod 4$, $n^6 - 2n^5 + 2^2 \cdot n^4 - 2^3 \cdot n^3 + 2^4 \cdot n^2 - 2^5 \cdot n + 2^6$ is congruent to 3 modulo 4. Since all the divisors of $m_1^2 + 1$ are congruent to 1 modulo 4, 11 must divide each of $n+2$ and $n^6 - 2n^5 + 2^2 \cdot n^4 - 2^3 \cdot n^3 + 2^4 \cdot n^2 - 2^5 \cdot n + 2^6$. But this is impossible, because $11 \mid (n+2)$ implies that $n \equiv -2 \pmod{11}$ and
$$
n^6 - 2n^5 + 2^2 \cdot n^4 - 2^3 \cdot n^3 + 2^4 \cdot n^2 - 2^5 \cdot n + 2^6 \equiv 7 \cdot 2^6 \not\equiv 0 \pmod{11}.
$$
Therefore, our assumption was wrong and $n^7 + 7$ is not a perfect square for any integer $n$.
