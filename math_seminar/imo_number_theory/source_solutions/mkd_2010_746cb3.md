## Supplied solution 1

Let $m$, $n$ and $k$ be positive integers and $p$ be a prime. Since $(n, k) = 1 \Leftrightarrow (n, n-k) = 1$, we have

$$
f(n) = \frac{n(n+1)}{2} - \frac{n \cdot \phi(n)}{2},
$$
where $\phi(n)$ is the Euler function.
If $m > n$, $(m, n) = 1$ and $f(m) = f(n)$, then
$$
m(m+1-\phi(m)) = n(n+1-\phi(n)) \Rightarrow m \le n,
$$
a contradiction. Therefore, if $f(n+p) = f(n)$, then $n = pk$ for some positive integer $k$ and
$$
(k+1)(pk + p + 1 - \phi(pk + p)) = k(pk + 1 - \phi(pk)).
$$
This implies that
$$
pk + 1 - \phi(pk) = a(k + 1) \quad (1)
$$
and
$$
pk + p + 1 - \phi(pk + p) = ak \quad (2)
$$
for some positive integer $a$. In particular,
$$
(p-a)k = a-1 + \phi(pk) \Rightarrow p > a \ge 1
$$
and
$$
a = \phi(pk + p) - \phi(pk) - p \equiv -1 \pmod{p-1}.
$$
So, we have $a = p-2$. Substituting this in (1) and (2) we obtain
$$
\phi(pk) = 2k + 3 - p \quad (3)
$$
and
$$
\phi(pk + p) = 2k + 1 + p \quad (4)
$$
We shall prove that $p \nmid k$ and $p \nmid k+1$. If $p \nmid k+1$, the relation (4) implies $p \nmid 1$, a contradiction. On the other hand if $p \nmid k$, then (3) implies $p \nmid 3$. Setting $k = 3^\alpha k_1$, where $k_1$ is an integer such that $3 \nmid k_1$, and substituting this in (3) we get $\phi(k_1) = k_1$. So $k_1 = 1$ and $k = 3^\alpha$. Now substituting this in (4) gives a contradiction. Using this observation, we obtain from (3) and (4) the equalities
$$
\phi(k) = \frac{2(k+1)}{p-1} - 1 \quad (5)
$$
and
$$
\phi(k+1) = \frac{2(k+1)}{p-1} + 1 \quad (6)
$$
In particular, $p \ge 5$ and $k+1 \ge 6$. Moreover $\phi(k+1) - \phi(k) = 2$ and either $4 \nmid \phi(k)$ or $4 \nmid \phi(k+1)$.
If $4 \nmid \phi(k)$, then $k = q^\beta$ or $k = 2q^\beta$, where $q$ is an odd prime and $\beta \ge 1$ is an integer. From the equality (5) we obtain
$$
\frac{2(k+1)}{p-1} - 1 = \phi(k) \ge \frac{1}{2} \left(1 - \frac{1}{q}\right) \cdot k \ge \frac{1}{2} \cdot \frac{2}{3} \cdot k = \frac{k}{3} \Rightarrow p \le 5 \Rightarrow p = 5.
$$
If $p=5$, from (5) we obtain the relation $\phi(k) = \frac{k-1}{2}$. So, $k$ must be odd, $k+1$
must be even and $\phi(k+1) \le \frac{k+1}{2}$. This contradicts the equality (6).
If $4 \nmid \phi(k+1)$, then $k+1 = q^\beta$ or $k+1 = 2q^\beta$, where $q$ is an odd prime and $\beta \ge 1$ is an integer. From the equality (6) we obtain
$$
\frac{2(k+1)}{p-1} + 1 = \phi(k+1) \ge \frac{k+1}{3}.
$$
If $p > 7$, then this implies
$$
\frac{6}{p-7} \ge \frac{2(k+1)}{p-1}
$$
and therefore by (5) we must have
$$
\phi(k) \le \frac{13-p}{p-7}
$$
Which is impossible. On the other hand, if $p \le 7$, then $p=7$ and, as $\frac{p-1}{2}$ divides $k+1$, we also have $q=3$. Using (6) again, we see that $k+1 = 2 \cdot 3^\alpha$ leads to a contradiction, and $k+1 = 3^\alpha$ leads to $k+1=3$, which was already ruled out.
