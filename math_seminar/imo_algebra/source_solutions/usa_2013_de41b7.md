## Supplied solution 1

**Lemma 2.** For any positive integer $m$, we have
$$
\sum_{x \in X,\ x \le m} \lfloor \sqrt[k]{\frac{m}{x}} \rfloor = m.
$$
*Proof.* Observe that every positive integer may be written as the product of an element of $X$ and a perfect $k^{\text{th}}$ power. Thus, we may divide the set $\{1, 2, \dots, m\}$ into the sets
$$
I_x = \{ k \mid k \le m,\ \frac{k}{x} \text{ is a perfect } k^{\text{th}} \text{ power} \},
$$
which have cardinality $|I_x| = \lfloor \sqrt[k]{m/x} \rfloor$. The desired equality then follows by computing the cardinality of $\{1, 2, \dots, m\}$ in two ways. $\square$


Now, let $1 = x_1 < x_2 < \dots$ be the elements of $X$. We claim by induction on $n$ that $a_n = x_n$. For the base case, we have $a_1 = x_1 = 1$. Now, suppose that $a_k = x_k$ for all $k < n$. By Lemma 2 and the inductive hypothesis, we see that
$$
x_n = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \lfloor \sqrt[k]{\frac{x_n}{x_i}} \rfloor = 1 + \sum_{i=1}^{n-1} \lfloor \sqrt[k]{\frac{x_n}{x_i}} \rfloor = 1 + \sum_{i=1}^{n-1} \lfloor \sqrt[k]{\frac{x_n}{a_i}} \rfloor.
$$
Because $x_n > x_{n-1} = x_{n-1}$, by the definition of $a_n$ we conclude that $a_n \le x_n$. On the other hand, if $a_n < x_n$, then the sum which Lemma 2 gives for $a_n$ contains at most $n-1$ terms. Therefore, we conclude by Lemma 2, the inductive hypothesis, and the definition of $a_n$ that
$$
a_n \le \sum_{i=1}^{n-1} \lfloor \sqrt[k]{\frac{a_n}{x_i}} \rfloor = \sum_{i=1}^{n-1} \lfloor \sqrt[k]{\frac{a_n}{a_i}} \rfloor = a_n - 1,
$$
which is a contradiction

## Supplied solution 2

**Solution 2 (By Ilya Bogdanov).** We again show that $\{a_1, a_2, \dots\}$ and the set $X$ in the previous solution coincide. For a positive integer $n$, consider the function
$$
f_n(x) = x - 1 - \sum_{i=1}^{n-1} \lfloor \sqrt[k]{\frac{x}{a_i}} \rfloor.
$$
Denote by $g_n(x)$ the number of indices $i \le n$ such that $\frac{x}{a_i}$ is a perfect $k^{\text{th}}$ power. Observe that for every $x \ge a_n$, we have
$$
f_n(x+1) - f_n(x) = 1 - g_n(x),
$$
which implies that $f_n(x) + 1 \ge f_n(x+1)$. Observe now that $f_n(a_{n-1}) = -1$ because $f_{n-1}(a_{n-1}) = 0$, so we may check by induction on $n$ that $f_n(x) < 0$ for integers $x$ satisfying $a_{n-1} \le x < a_n$. Now, if $g_n(x) > 0$, then $f_n(x) \le f_n(x-1)$, which implies that $x \ne a_n$ and therefore that $\frac{a_j}{a_i}$ is never a perfect $k^{\text{th}}$ power of an integer for $j > i$.
We are now ready to prove the claim by induction on $n$. For the base case, we have $a_1 = x_1 = 1$. Suppose now that the elements of $X$ which are at most $a_n$ are exactly $a_1, a_2, \dots, a_n$. Let $b$ be the least integer greater than $a_n$ such that $g_n(b) = 0$. We claim that $b = a_{n+1}$ and that $b = x_{n+1}$.
For the first claim, notice that all numbers of the form $\frac{a_j}{a_i}$ with $1 \le i < j \le n$ are not $k^{\text{th}}$ powers of rational numbers because $a_i$ and $a_j$ are themselves not divisible by $k^{\text{th}}$ powers of primes. Therefore, for each integer $a_n < m < b$, there is exactly one index $i \le n$ such that $\frac{m}{a_i}$ is the $k^{\text{th}}$ power of an integer. This implies that $f_{n+1}(m) = f_{n+1}(m-1)$ for each such $m$, so $f_{n+1}(b-1) = f_{n+1}(a_n) = -1$. Because $\frac{b}{a_i}$ is not a $k^{\text{th}}$ power of an integer for any $i$, we have $f_{n+1}(b) = f_{n+1}(b-1) + 1 = 0$, hence $b = a_{n+1}$.
For the second claim, we have already shown that all integers between $a_n$ and $b$ are divisible by the $k^{\text{th}}$ power of some prime. Therefore, to show that $b = x_{n+1}$, it suffices to show that $b$ is not divisible by the $k^{\text{th}}$ power of a prime. Suppose otherwise for the sake of contradiction, and let $y > 1$ be the greatest integer such that $y^k \mid b$. By construction, $\frac{b}{y^k}$ is not divisible by the $k^{\text{th}}$ power of a prime, meaning that $\frac{b}{y^k} = a_i$ for some $i \le n$. But this implies that $\frac{b}{a_i}$ is a $k^{\text{th}}$ power of an integer, which contradicts the fact that $g_n(b) = 0$.
We have thus shown that $b = a_{n+1} = x_{n+1}$, completing the induction.
