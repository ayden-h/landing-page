## Supplied solution 1

To prove $M = p^a q^b r^c$ is necessary, notice that if one initially places fewer than $p^a q^b r^c$ pieces at $(a, b, c)$, then the goal cannot be achieved. Indeed, let the weight of a game piece $u$ at $(x, y, z)$ be $w(u) = \frac{1}{p^x q^y r^z}$. According to the rule, before and after each move, the total weight of all game pieces $W = w(u)$ does not change. To have a game piece at $(0,0,0)$, we must have $W \ge 1$; but $W < 1$ for $p^a q^b r^c$ pieces at $(a, b, c)$, a contradiction.

It requires to prove $M = p^a q^b r^c$ is sufficient. We give a more general result on the $n$-dimensional game board: let $a_1, \dots, a_n, p_1, \dots, p_n$ be non-negative integers, satisfying $2 \le p_1 \le \dots \le p_n$. Consider the game on
$$
Q(a_1, \dots, a_n) = \{(x_1, \dots, x_n) \in \mathbb{Z}^n \mid 0 \le x_i \le a_i, i = 1, \dots, n\}.
$$
Initially, put $M$ game pieces on $Q(a_1, \dots, a_n)$. In each move, one can choose a position, remove $p_i$ ($1 \le i \le n$) pieces, and place one piece at the preceding position along the $x_i$ direction. This is called an "$X_i$ move". If one chooses a position and just remove $q$ pieces, then it is called a "$T_q$ move". For $n=0$, $Q = \{0\}$, there is only one type of possible moves, which is the $T_q$ move.

Divide the board into Zone-I as $Q(a_1, \dots, a_n - 1)$ and Zone-II as $Q(a_1, \dots, a_{n-1}) \times \{a_n\} = Q(a_1, \dots, a_n) \setminus Q(a_1, \dots, a_{n-1})$. Let $M$ and $K$ be respectively the number of pieces and the number of positions with at least one piece in $Q(a_1, \dots, a_n)$. Let $M_1, K_1$ and $M_2, K_2$ be respectively the $M, K$ values in Zone-I and Zone-II. In the following argument, the key idea is to take as many $X_n$ moves as possible in Zone-II, so as to have more pieces in Zone-I. At the end of the process, there are at most $p_n - 1$ pieces at each of the $K_2$ positions (initially there are pieces on them) in Zone-II, and at least $\frac{M_2 - (p_n - 1)K_2}{p_n}$ pieces are added to Zone-I. We call this sequence of moves as "transmission from Zone-II to Zone-I".

We shall justify the following three claims.

**Claim** $A(n)$ if $M = p_1^{a_1} \cdots p_n^{a_n}$, then a sequence of moves can make one piece at the origin.

**Claim B**($n$) let $u \ge 2$ be an integer, if $M + K > u \cdot p_1^{a_1} \cdots p_n^{a_n}$, then a sequence of $X_1, \dots, X_n$ moves can make $u$ pieces at the origin.

**Claim C($n$)** let $q \ge p_n$ and $s$ be positive integers, $t$ be a nonnegative integer, if $M + K > sq \cdot p_1^{a_1} \dots p_n^{a_n} + t$, then one can first take $\lfloor \frac{t}{q} \rfloor$ of $T_q$ moves, then take a sequence of $X_1, \dots, X_n$ moves to put $sq$ pieces at the origin.

Apply induction on $n$. Obviously, $A(0)$, $B(0)$, $C(0)$ hold. Suppose we have $A(n-1)$, $B(n-1)$, $C(n-1)$. Now induct on $a_n$: when $a_n = 0$, $Q(a_1, \dots, a_n) = Q(a_1, \dots, a_{n-1}) \times \{0\}$, the claims $A(n)$, $B(n)$, $C(n)$ on $Q(a_1, \dots, a_n)$ correspond to $A(n-1)$, $B(n-1)$, $C(n-1)$ on $Q(a_1, \dots, a_{n-1})$, respectively, and they are already justified by the induction hypothesis. In the following, consider the situations when $a_n \ge 1$.

**Proof of** $A(n)$

a.1 If $M_1 \ge K_2$, one can transmit at least $\frac{M_2 - (p_n - 1)K_2}{p_n}$ pieces from Zone-II to Zone-I, after which the number of pieces in Zone-I is
$$
\begin{aligned}
M_1' &\ge M_1 + \frac{M_2 - (n-1)K_2}{p_n} \\
&= p_1^{a_1} \cdots p_{n-1}^{a_{n-1}} p_n^{a_n} + \frac{(p_n - 1)(M_1 - K_2)}{p_n} \\
&\ge p_1^{a_1} \cdots p_{n-1}^{a_{n-1}} p_n^{a_n}.
\end{aligned}
$$
In Zone-I, by the induction hypothesis, one can eventually put one piece at the origin.

a.2 If $M_1 < K_2$, then $M_2 + K_2 > M_2 + M_1 = p_n^{a_n} \cdot p_1^{a_1} \cdots p_{n-1}^{a_{n-1}}$. Notice that $p_n^{a_n} \ge 2$. According to $B(n-1)$, in Zone-II one can transmit $p_n^{a_n}$ pieces to $(0, \dots, 0, a_n)$, then just take $X_n$ moves to put one piece at the origin.

**Proof of** $B(n)$

b.1 If $M_1 + K_1 \ge \frac{p_n}{p_n-1}K_2$, one can transmit at least $\frac{M_2 - (p_n - 1)K_2}{p_n}$ pieces from Zone-II to Zone-I, after which
$$
\begin{aligned}
M_1' + K_1' &\ge M_1 + K_1 + \frac{M_2 - (p_n - 1)K_2}{p_n} \\
&> M_1 + K_1 + \frac{u \cdot p_1^{a_1} \cdots p_n^{a_n} - M_1 - K_1 - p_n K_2}{p_n}
\end{aligned}
$$
$$
\begin{align*}
&= u \cdot p_1^{a_1} \cdots p_{n-1}^{a_{n-1}} p_n^{a_n - 1} + \frac{(p_n - 1)(M_1 + K_1) - p_n K_2}{p_n} \\
&\ge u \cdot p_1^{a_1} \cdots p_{n-1}^{a_{n-1}} p_n^{a_n - 1}.
\end{align*}
$$
In Zone-I, by the induction hypothesis, one can eventually put $u$ pieces at the origin.

b.2 If $M_1 + K_1 < \frac{p_n}{p_n - 1} K_2$, by Bernoulli's inequality,
$$
M_1 + K_1 < \frac{p_n}{p_n - 1} K_2 \le 2(1 + a_1) \dots (1 + a_{n-1}) \le p_n^{a_n} p_1^{a_1} \dots p_{n-1}^{a_{n-1}},
$$
which implies that $p_1^{a_1} \dots p_n^{a_n} - M_1 - K_1$ is nonnegative. Since
$$
\begin{align*}
M_2 + K_2 &> u \cdot p_1^{a_1} \cdots p_n^{a_n} - (M_1 + K_1) \\
&= (u-1)p_n^{a_n} \cdot p_1^{a_1} \cdots p_{n-1}^{a_{n-1}} + (p_1^{a_1} \cdots p_n^{a_n} - M_1 - K_1),
\end{align*}
$$
and $u-1 \ge 1$, $p_n \ge p_{n-1}$, in Zone-II we may use the conclusion of $C(n-1)$ to take $\left\lfloor \frac{p_1^{a_1} \cdots p_n^{a_n} - M_1 - K_1}{p_n} \right\rfloor$ of $T_{p_n}$ moves, then transmit $(u-1)p_n^{a_n}$ pieces to $(0, \dots, 0, a_n)$. The latter $(u-1)p_n^{a_n}$ pieces at $(0, \dots, 0, a_n)$ can contribute $u-1$ pieces at the origin by $X_n$ moves. The former pieces removed by $\left\lfloor \frac{p_1^{a_1} \cdots p_n^{a_n} - M_1 - K_1}{p_n} \right\rfloor$ of $T_{p_n}$ moves can be transmitted by $X_n$ moves into $\left\lfloor \frac{p_1^{a_1} \cdots p_n^{a_n} - M_1 - K_1}{p_n} \right\rfloor$ pieces in Zone-I. After that, in Zone-I, there are
$$
\begin{align*}
M_1' &\ge M_1 + \left\lfloor \frac{p_1^{a_1} \cdots p_n^{a_n} - M_1 - K_1}{p_n} \right\rfloor \\
&= p_1^{a_1} \cdots p_{n-1}^{a_{n-1}} p_n^{a_n - 1} + \left\lfloor \frac{(p_n - 1) M_1 - K_1}{p_n} \right\rfloor \\
&\ge p_1^{a_1} \cdots p_{n-1}^{a_{n-1}} p_n^{a_n - 1}
\end{align*}
$$
pieces. Due to $A(n)$, eventually one piece can be placed at the origin. Altogether, $u$ pieces at the origin.

**Proof of** $C(n)$

c.1 If $t < q$, no $T_q$ move is needed. Notice that $sq \ge 2$ and
$$
M + K < sq \cdot p_1^{a_1} \dots p_n^{a_n} + t \ge sq \cdot p_1^{a_1} \dots p_n^{a_n}.
$$
By the result of $B(n)$, $sq$ pieces can be transmitted to the origin.

c.2 If $t \ge q$, then some $T_q$ moves are needed. If there are more than $q$ pieces at a place of Zone-II, take a $T_q$ move, after which $M' = M - q$, $K' = K$, and the situation becomes $t' = t - q$. After a few $T_q$ moves, it becomes either (c.1), $t < q$; or the number of pieces at every position of Zone-II is less than or equal to $q$. We only need to handle the latter case. Suppose there are $L$ positions in Zone-II that contain exactly $q$ pieces. By Bernoulli's inequality,
$$
\begin{align*} 
M_1 + K_1 &= M + K - (M_2 + K_2) > sq \cdot p_1^{a_1} \dots p_n^{a_n} \\ 
&\quad + t - (q(K_2 - L) + (q+1)L) \\ 
&= sq \cdot p_1^{a_1} \dots p_{n-1}^{a_{n-1}} p_n^{a_n} + t \\ 
&\quad + (sq \cdot p_1^{a_1} \dots p_{n-1}^{a_{n-1}} p_n^{a_n-1}(p_n - 1) - qK_2 - L) \\ 
&\ge sq \cdot p_1^{a_1} \dots p_{n-1}^{a_{n-1}} p_n^{a_n} + t \\ 
&\quad + (q(1+a_1) \dots (1+a_{n-1}) - qK_2 - L) \\ 
&\ge sq \cdot p_1^{a_1} \dots p_{n-1}^{a_{n-1}} p_n^{a_n} + t - L. 
\end{align*}
$$
As $q \ge p_n$, take $X_n$ move once at each of these $L$ positions with $q$ pieces. After that,
$$
M'_1 + K'_1 \ge M_1 + K_1 + L > sq \cdot p_1^{a_1} \dots p_{n-1}^{a_{n-1}} p_n^{a_n} + t,
$$
by the induction hypothesis applied to Zone-I, $\lfloor \frac{t}{q} \rfloor$ of $T_q$ moves followed by a sequence of $X_1, \dots, X_n$ moves can send $sq$ pieces to the origin.

This justifies $A(n)$, $B(n)$, $C(n)$, and also the problem statement.

## Supplied solution 2

(organized from Feng Chenxu's solution). The argument for $M \ge p^a q^b r^c$ is the same. We shall prove $M = p^a q^b r^c$ satisfies the problem condition.

**Lemma** For a path $A_1, A_2, \dots, A_t$ in $Q$, $A_i$ ($i = 2, \dots, t$) has two coordinates equal to those of $A_{i-1}$ and one coordinate equal to one plus that of $A_{i-1}$. Assign each $A_i$ a number $a_i$ such that $a_1 = 1$, $a_{i+1} = \frac{a_i}{x_i}$, where
$$
x_i = \begin{cases} p, & \text{if } x(A_{i+1}) = x(A_i) + 1, \\ q, & \text{if } y(A_{i+1}) = y(A_i) + 1, \\ r, & \text{if } z(A_{i+1}) = z(A_i) + 1. \end{cases}
$$
If there are $b_i$ pieces on $A_i$ and $\sum_{i=1}^t b_i a_i \ge 1$, then a sequence of moves can send one piece to $A_1$.

**Proof of lemma** Every time, remove pieces on $A_{i+1}$ and place a piece on $A_i$ for some $i$, until no more move is possible. During the process, $\sum_{i=1}^t b_i a_i$ does not change. Suppose at the end there is no piece on $A_1$. There are at most $x_i - 1$ pieces on $A_{i+1}$ or else another move can be made from $A_{i+1}$ to $A_i$. It follows that $b_{i+1} a_{i+1} \le (x_i - 1) a_{i+1} = a_i - a_{i+1}$, and
$$
\sum_{i=1}^{t} b_i a_i \le \sum_{i=1}^{t} a_{i-1} - a_i = a_1 - a_t < 1
$$
is a contradiction. The lemma is verified.

For the original problem, let $f(x, y, z)$ be the number of pieces at $(x, y, z)$ in the initial state.

First, consider $c = 0$. Apply induction on $a+b$. Assume $a \le b$. When $a+b=0$, the conclusion is obvious. For $a=0$, $b>0$, keep taking (2) moves (recall that (1)–(3) are the three types of legal moves described in the problem, in the $x$, $y$, and $z$ directions, respectively) until there is no more move. By that time, at $(0, i, 0)$, $0 \le i \le b-1$, there are at least
$$
\left\lfloor \frac{f(0, b, 0)}{q} \right\rfloor + M - f(0, b, 0) = \frac{M}{q} + \left\lfloor \left(1 - \frac{1}{q}\right) (M - f(0, b, 0)) \right\rfloor \ge \frac{M}{q}
$$
pieces. The conclusion follows from the induction hypothesis. For $a=1$, $b=1$, assume $f(0, 0, 0) = 0$, and let $f(0, 1, 0) = a$, $f(1, 0, 0) = b$. Then
$$
f(1, 1, 0) = pq - a - b \ge \min\{pq - pa, pq - qb\}.
$$
Assume $pq - a - b \ge pq - pa$. Take (1) move $q - a$ times on $(1, 1, 0)$, followed by (2) move once on $(0, 1, 0)$. Now there is a piece at the origin.

For $a+b \ge 3$, $b \ge 2$. Assume there are $t$ pieces in the region $0 \le y \le b-1$. Take (2) moves at every position with $y=b$ until no more move is possible, at which time there are at most $q-1$ pieces at every position. So, at least $\left\lfloor \frac{M-t-(a+1)(q-1)}{q} \right\rfloor$ moves are taken. (i) If $t \ge a+1$, then in the region $0 \le y \le b-1$ the number of pieces is at least $\left\lfloor \frac{M-t-(a+1)(q-1)}{q} \right\rfloor + t = \left\lfloor \frac{M+(t-a-1)(q-1)}{q} \right\rfloor \ge \frac{M}{q}$. By the induction hypothesis, another sequence of moves can send one piece to (0, 0, 0). (ii) If $t \le a$, in the same manner, assume at most $b$ pieces in the region $0 \le x \le a-1$. Then $f(a, b, 0) \ge M-a-b$.

If no piece exists on the line $y=0$, then there are $M$ pieces in the region $1 \le y \le b$. Using the induction hypothesis for $q$ times, we infer that a sequence of moves can send $q$ pieces to $(0, 1, 0)$. After that, one (2) move will send a piece to $(0, 0, 0)$.

If there are pieces on the line $y=0$, say at $A$. Let $B = (a, b, c)$. There is a path through the origin, $A$, and $B$; moreover, the number assigned to $A$ is $\ge \frac{1}{p^a}$, and the number assigned to $B$ is $\frac{1}{p^a q^b}$. We have
$$
\frac{1}{p^a} + \frac{f(a,b,0)}{p^a q^b} \ge \frac{M}{p^a q^b} + \frac{1}{p^a} - \frac{a+b}{p^a q^b}.
$$
Since $a \le b, b \ge 2$, it follows that $a+b \le q^b$, $\frac{M}{p^a q^b} \ge 1$, and thus $\frac{1}{p^a} + \frac{f(a,b,0)}{p^a q^b} \ge 1$. According to the lemma, a sequence of moves can send a piece to (0, 0, 0), which validates the conclusion for $c=0$. By symmetry, the conclusion holds if one of $a, b, c$ is 0.
